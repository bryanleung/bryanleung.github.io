QuintAce Communities

From AI-generated layouts to a working community experience

Summary

Goal

Enable player-run communities where members can play together, share posts, and compete.

My role

Set lobby priorities, explored six layouts with AI, designed the final experience, specified responsive behavior, and QA’d staging.

Key decision

Separated joining community tables from community activity, keeping the lobby focused on getting players into a game.

Result

The team reached dev-complete on staging in under a week. Not yet released; no player outcomes measured.

The brief

QuintAce’s Compete mode already included public Leagues. Communities would add player-run groups where members could play together, share posts, and compete on a weekly leaderboard.

The business goals were to increase social play and give professional-player sponsors a place to earn through lessons and training.

This was also the team’s first feature built with AI tools. No feature success metric was set, and QuintAce had no active players yet. The project tested delivery speed, not demand.

My contribution

The product manager drafted the PRD with Claude Code. I generated six low-fidelity layouts in Figma, helped select a direction with product and design, and rebuilt it using our design system.

Approximately two engineers implemented the feature with Claude Code. I reviewed staging, logged defects in Jira, and the engineers corrected them.

Exploring the structure

The six layouts explored one question: how should Leagues and Communities share a lobby?

Options included a mode picker, dual panels, a mixed table list, a bottom drawer, side navigation, and a carousel.

AI provided structurally different starting points, but several had weak hierarchy or overloaded layouts. The teams chose Side Nav because its three-column structure also suited the community pages and used a conventional layout.

Prioritizing play

The generated layout put rank points in the center and Communities behind a small link. I reworked it around the lobby’s primary task: getting players to a table.

  • Put the live table and Join Table action in the center.
  • Moved weekly statistics into a bottom drawer.
  • Placed members and available tables on the left.
  • Placed community information and the leaderboard on the right.

With the product team, I also gave Communities its own tab beside Leagues and Friends.

AI output stopped at the wireframe. I built the final screens using our design-system components.

Separating play from community activity

I split the experience into two areas:

A Play at Community Tables action connects the community area back to the lobby.

The overview includes a creator welcome, a hand of the week selected by Quint, the app’s AI coach, and member progress. The feed supports messages, hands, drills, links, images, likes, and comments.

Decisions beyond the layout

Make community switching visible

I compared a dropdown with a horizontal community list.

I chose the list because it exposes the ability to switch communities. The trade-off is scale: players can currently join three communities, while a dropdown would accommodate more.

Define entry and membership states

I specified behavior for key states:

  • No community joined: open Discover.
  • Existing membership: open the last active community.
  • Three-community limit reached: block another join and explain why.
  • Leaving a community: ask for confirmation.

The specified behavior also deletes a community when its admin leaves.

Specify content and responsive behavior

I defined image layouts from one image through six or more, with the final tile showing the remaining count.

Community creation uses a name, description, and one of 37 preset logos. I specified the flows for desktop, tablet, and mobile.

Checking the implementation

Staging QA uncovered problems that were not apparent in the static designs:

  • Incorrect responsive breakpoints.
  • Nested scrolling on mobile.
  • Incorrect selection of desktop and mobile leaderboard variants.
  • Incorrect behavior in the statistics drawer and community picker.

I logged the issues in Jira for engineering to fix.

Outcome

Dev-complete on staging in under a week

AI helped produce the first PRD draft, layout options, and much of the implementation. Existing Leagues functionality and design-system components also contributed to the speed.

My contribution was setting priorities, reworking the layout, defining edge states and responsive behavior, and checking the working build.

The feature had not been released. There was no player data, so this result demonstrates delivery speed, not adoption or product effectiveness.

Reflection

AI was useful for getting past the blank page. The generated layouts still needed hierarchy, interaction decisions, and detailed specifications.

Next time, I would check breakpoints, scrolling, and component variants earlier.

I would also define product evaluation before release, starting with whether players join a community and return to its tables. Reaching staging quickly does not establish that players want the feature.
