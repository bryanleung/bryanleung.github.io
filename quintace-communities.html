QuintAce Communities

A community feature for a poker app, taken from PRD to dev-complete on staging in under a week. The team's first feature built with AI tools.

Product
QuintAce
Role
Senior Product Designer
Scope
AI layout exploration, design direction, final design, QA
Timeline
Under a week
Year
2026

Summary

Goal
Increase social play, and give sponsors (professional poker players) a place to earn from lessons and training.
Experiment
The team's first feature built with AI tools, mainly Claude Code, to see how fast a whole feature could reach staging.
Role
I generated six layouts with AI, set the lobby's priorities, and finished the design in our design system. The direction was chosen with the product team.
Result
Dev-complete on staging in under a week, where I would have estimated two to three months. It isn't released, so there is no player data.

The brief

QuintAce's Compete mode already had public Leagues. Communities add a player-run layer on top:

  • Create a community, or discover and join one.
  • Play at community tables with its members.
  • Post to a message board and comment.
  • Compete on a weekly community leaderboard.

It was also a test of how far AI could shorten the path from an idea to working software. No success metric was set for the feature itself.

How the week ran

  1. PRD. Generated by the product manager with Claude Code.
  2. Priorities. The lobby's primary action is getting players to a table. Community actions belong in the Communities tab.
  3. Explorations. I generated six lo-fi layouts into Figma with a text-to-Figma skill, prompted to explore layout ideas.
  4. Direction. The product and design teams chose the Side Nav layout. I rebuilt it with our existing design system.
  5. Build. About two engineers built it with Claude Code. I QA'd it on staging and logged what was built wrong in Jira for them to fix.

Exploring with AI

The six layouts each answer one question, how Leagues and Communities should share a lobby.

The other three were a bottom drawer, a side navigation and a carousel. AI gave me range, six structurally different options to compare before any polish.

Most were text-heavy, with a confusing visual hierarchy, as Dual Panel shows. Bottom Drawer packed in more than a drawer is meant to hold.

The chosen direction

The teams chose Side Nav. Its three columns also suited the community pages, and it is a conventional layout that players already know from apps like Facebook.

What I changed from the AI version

  • One main action. Getting to a table is what the lobby is for, so the live table and its Join Table button take the center, with nothing competing for attention. The rank points number came out of the middle, and weekly stats moved into a drawer at the bottom.
  • Members and the community up front. Members you can join sit above the community tables on the left. The community switcher and the community's own card sit on the right, above the weekly leaderboard.
  • Communities as a tab. The AI version reached communities through a small link. With the product team, we gave Communities its own tab beside Leagues and Friends, so each has its own view.

I also split the feature in two. In the Compete lobby, a Communities tab beside Leagues and Friends is only for getting to a table, with tables on the left, the live table in the middle and the leaderboard on the right. Everything else about a community lives in its own Communities tab.

AI output stopped at the wireframe. The final screens are built from our design system components, and the decisions below were mine.

The Communities tab

This is where a community lives between games. One button, Play at Community Tables, leads back to the lobby.

Overview

A welcome from the creator, a hand of the week picked by Quint (the app's AI coach) from community play, and member progress such as league promotions, leaderboard wins and badges.

Feed

The message board. Members post a message, a hand, a drill or a link, and others like and comment.

Screenshots in posts

Posts and comments can carry images. I set the layout rules for one image up to six or more, where the last tile shows the remaining count.

Decisions made by hand

Switching communities

I compared a dropdown with a horizontal list. The list shows upfront that you can switch communities, where a dropdown hides it. The trade-off is scale. Players can join three communities today, and a dropdown would handle more.

Rules for the edges

  • No community yet: the tab opens Discover.
  • Already joined: it opens the last active community.
  • At the limit of three: joining another is blocked, with the reason shown.
  • Leaving asks for confirmation. If the admin leaves, the community is deleted.

A logo without an upload

Creating a community takes a name, a description and a logo picked from a preset set of 37.

Three screen sizes

Every flow is specified for desktop, tablet and mobile.

What the AI got wrong

The engineers built with Claude Code. When the build reached staging, I QA'd it and logged each wrong interaction in Jira, and the engineers fixed them. The errors were in the details.

  • Breakpoints. The breakpoints between screen sizes were wrong.
  • Double scrolling. On mobile, panels scrolled inside a page that also scrolled.
  • Two versions of one component. The leaderboard is designed differently on desktop and on mobile, and the AI could not tell which design to implement where.
  • Micro-interactions. Many were wrong, including the stats drawer at the bottom of the lobby and the community picker.

Outcome

PRD to dev-complete on staging
Under a week
My estimate for a feature this size
2 to 3 months
  • What AI did: drafted the PRD (the product manager, with Claude Code), produced six layout options (I ran a text-to-Figma skill), and wrote much of the code (the engineers, with Claude Code).
  • What I did: set the priorities, reworked the chosen layout, built the final screens in the design system, defined the edge states and responsive specs, and QA'd the build on staging. The direction and the Communities tab were decided with the product team.
  • Why it was fast: AI helped, and so did reuse. Leagues and our design system already existed, and much of the lobby is built on them. The two to three months is my own estimate, not a measured baseline.
  • Not measured: anything about players, because QuintAce has no active players yet. Sponsors have been onboarded by marketing. Dev-complete on staging is not a release.

Reflection

  • The time saved was in the blank-page stages: the first draft of the PRD and the first set of layouts. The decisions still took judgment.
  • AI was weakest in details such as breakpoints, scrolling, and choosing between two versions of one component. Those are the places I would check first next time.
  • Speed to staging says nothing about whether players want communities. Next I would define that measure, such as the share of players who join a community and come back to its tables.
