Selective Modules Release

Simplifying assignment without losing instructor control

Assigning ten course materials to the same students required repeating a workflow ten times, approximately 150 clicks. I designed module-level assignment so instructors could assign the collection in 6.

The interface change was small. The harder problem was defining how module-level assignments should interact with existing assignments on individual materials.

Product
Canvas LMS
Role
Senior Product Designer
Team
One product manager and six engineers
Scope
Design, research, and cross-functional workshops
Year
2024

The challenge

A repeated task that belonged at a higher level

Instructors need to give different students different materials: additional practice for an advanced student, remedial activities for another group, or content intended for a particular course section.

Canvas supported this one material at a time. Assigning one item took approximately 15 clicks across three pages. Assigning ten materials to the same students meant repeating that work ten times.

Instructors already organized related materials into modules. The opportunity was to make that existing structure assignable, rather than ask instructors to manage each item separately.

It would be great if I could give Modules unique gating rules specific to individual students or groups or sections within the course, similar to how I can create unique assignment due dates for different students and sections.

The central design problem was inheritance

Assigning a module could eliminate repetitive work, but it introduced a conflict: an item inside that module might already have its own assignment.

Before designing the screens, we needed to decide which setting should take precedence.

A module-level change could simplify management while also overwriting an instructor’s deliberate exception. Preserving every exception, however, meant a module would not necessarily have one uniform assignment.

The goal was to reduce repetitive work without silently undoing instructors’ choices.

My role

I facilitated workshops with the product manager and engineers to establish a shared understanding of the problem before choosing an interface.

I then:

  • Prototyped two approaches to assignment.
  • Wrote and built an unmoderated test in Maze.
  • Interpreted the results alongside implementation and interaction trade-offs.
  • Recommended reusing the existing Assign To tray.
  • Proposed assignment-precedence rules and worked through edge cases with product and engineering.

The product manager distributed the test through the Canvas Community. Another designer identified the existing tray pattern elsewhere in Canvas, giving us an alternative to introducing a new component.

Choosing an interaction pattern

Two approaches

I explored two ways for instructors to assign content.

What we tested

Participants received the same task: assign an assignment to a student. A single link randomly directed each participant to one of the two prototypes.

This test compared the assignment interactions. It did not establish whether instructors understood module inheritance or the behavior of item-level exceptions.

Interpreting the results

The tray had a higher reported success rate and a shorter average completion time. It also had more misclicks.

I did not treat the approximately seven-percentage-point success difference as conclusive. The test provided directional evidence, not proof that the tray was the better experience.

I recommended the tray based on the combined trade-offs:

  • It preserved context. Instructors could keep the Modules page visible while assigning content.
  • It reused an existing pattern. We could extend a component whose accessibility behavior had already been worked through.
  • It reduced implementation effort. The engineering manager estimated that the modal would require approximately one additional sprint.

The higher misclick rate remained unresolved. I did not inspect the click maps closely enough to determine whether it reflected confusion or exploration. That limited how confidently I could interpret the tray’s faster completion time.

Defining the product behavior

1. Assign the collection, not each item

The primary change was to make the module assignable.

Instead of assigning each material separately, instructors could select recipients once at the module level. Materials inside the module would inherit that assignment, subject to item-level exceptions.

This removed repeated assignment work when the same recipients needed the whole collection.

2. Preserve explicit item-level decisions

I proposed that an item’s own assignment should take precedence over its module’s assignment.

An instructor who deliberately changes an individual material is expressing a more specific intention. A later module-level setting should not silently erase that choice.

The product manager and engineering manager agreed with this approach.

This decision established a hierarchy:

  • The module provides the shared assignment.
  • An explicit item-level assignment takes precedence.
  • The module does not overwrite a deliberate item-level exception.

The trade-off is that assigning a module does not guarantee every item has identical recipients. Preserving control makes visibility into those exceptions important.

3. Keep dates on individual materials

We separated who receives content from when that content is due or available.

Due dates and availability can differ by material and by student. Supporting those settings at the module level would have added complexity, and instructors confirmed they did not need it there.

Module assignment therefore answered one question: who should receive this collection?

Dates remained on individual materials.

This kept the module-level interaction focused rather than turning it into a second place to manage every assignment setting.

4. Shorten the path to individual assignment

Module assignment addressed the repeated-work problem, but instructors still needed to manage individual materials.

We also brought Assign To closer to the work by making it available from the Modules page and the assignment Summary page, rather than requiring instructors to navigate three pages deep.

The two changes served different needs:

  • Module assignment reduced repetition across a collection.
  • Additional entry points reduced navigation for individual changes.

Working through exceptions

Most of the complexity sat in the rules, not the screens.

I documented edge cases involving module, section, and student assignments, proposed behavior for each, and reviewed them with the product manager and engineers.

Where we lacked evidence, we made judgment calls rather than treating assumptions as validated findings.

One unresolved issue was visibility. The project did not include a Modules-page overview showing who was assigned to what. That remained an important next problem, particularly because item-level exceptions could differ from the module’s assignment.

Outcome

From 150 clicks to 6 in the compared workflow

For ten materials already grouped in one module and intended for the same students:

That is a 96% reduction in clicks for this scenario.

This was a workflow comparison, not a measured reduction across all assignment activity. It did not establish adoption, time saved in live courses, or improved student outcomes.

What I could verify

The redesigned workflow substantially reduced the actions required to assign a collection to the same recipients.

What I could not verify

The feature shipped after I left Instructure, so I did not measure post-launch usage or effectiveness.

Instructor feedback before release indicated demand, but it was not evidence that the shipped experience succeeded:

I can’t wait to see this feature enabled! I have been hoping for a feature like this since I started using Canvas back in 2020.

For a post-launch evaluation, I would start with the share of courses assigning modules to specific students or sections. I would pair that with research into whether instructors understood inheritance and could verify the resulting access correctly.

Reflection

Define the decision criteria before testing

I would establish the sample-size rationale and decision criteria before running the comparison. That would make it clearer whether the study was intended to select an approach or provide one input into a broader product decision.

Investigate contradictory signals

The tray’s higher misclick rate deserved follow-up before the decision. Reviewing click maps would have helped distinguish harmless exploration from an interaction problem.

Test the rules, not only the controls

The interaction comparison addressed how instructors assigned a material. A stronger evaluation would also test whether they could predict what happened when module assignments and item-level exceptions conflicted.

Make exceptions visible

Preserving item-level control was intentional. But control is harder to use when instructors cannot easily inspect the resulting assignments.

The next design problem was therefore not another shortcut. It was helping instructors understand and verify who could access what.
