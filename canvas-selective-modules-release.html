Selective Modules Release

Simplifying assignment without losing instructor control

Summary

Goal

Reduce the repetitive work of assigning course materials to specific students or sections.

My role

Facilitated team workshops, prototyped and tested two approaches, and proposed assignment rules with product and engineering.

Key decision

Make whole modules assignable while preserving explicit item-level assignments, so module changes do not overwrite instructors’ deliberate exceptions.

Result

Reduced the compared workflow for assigning ten materials from approximately 150 clicks to six. Shipped after I left; post-launch usage was not measured.

Product
Canvas LMS
Role
Senior Product Designer
Team
One product manager and six engineers
Scope
Design, research, and cross-functional workshops
Year
2024

The problem

Canvas assigned materials to specific students one item at a time, about 15 clicks each. Ten materials took about 150. The fix ranked in the top five of a Canvas Community feature survey.

What I did

I ran workshops with product and engineering, then tested two prototypes in Maze with 54 participants. The existing Assign To tray beat a new permission modal on success (68.6% vs 61.8%) and speed (41 vs 59 seconds), and saved about a sprint.

The solution

Instructors assign a whole module once and every item inherits it. An item’s own assignment still wins, so module changes never erase a deliberate exception. Assign To also moved onto the Modules page.

Outcome

Ten materials went from about 150 clicks to 6. It shipped after I left Instructure, so adoption wasn’t measured.

The challenge

A repeated task that belonged at a higher level

Instructors often give different students different materials, such as extra practice for an advanced student, remedial work for a group, or content for one section.

Canvas handled this one material at a time. Each took about 15 clicks across three pages, so ten materials meant doing it all ten times.

Instructors already grouped related materials into modules. Making the module assignable would let them do it once.

It would be great if I could give Modules unique gating rules specific to individual students or groups or sections within the course, similar to how I can create unique assignment due dates for different students and sections.

It wasn’t one instructor’s wish. In a Canvas Community prioritization survey, Selective Modules Release ranked in the top five.

The central design problem was inheritance

An item inside a module might already have its own assignment. Overwriting it would undo an instructor’s deliberate choice. Keeping it meant a module wouldn’t always have one uniform assignment.

We had to decide which one wins before designing any screens.

My role

I ran workshops with the product manager and engineers to agree on the problem before choosing an interface. From there, I prototyped two approaches, built an unmoderated test in Maze, recommended the existing Assign To tray, and defined the precedence rules with product and engineering.

The product manager shared the test through the Canvas Community. Another designer pointed us to the tray pattern already used elsewhere in Canvas.

Choosing an interaction pattern

Two approaches

I prototyped two ways to assign content.

What we tested

Customer demand had already settled whether to build this. The study asked a narrower question, which pattern made assignment easier to complete.

Everyone got the same task, assigning an assignment to a student. A single link randomly opened one of the two prototypes.

Interpreting the results

The tray had a higher success rate and a faster average time, but more misclicks. I read that as a direction, not proof, and recommended the tray for three reasons.

  • It kept context. The Modules page stayed visible while assigning.
  • It reused a pattern. The component’s accessibility was already worked through.
  • It cost less to build. The engineering manager estimated the modal would take about one more sprint.

Defining the product behavior

1. Assign the collection, not each item

Instructors pick recipients once, at the module level, and every material inside inherits them.

2. Preserve explicit item-level decisions

I proposed that an item’s own assignment wins over its module’s. Changing a single material is a more specific decision, and a later module change shouldn’t silently erase it. The product manager and engineering manager agreed.

The cost is that a module’s items won’t always share the same recipients.

3. Keep dates on individual materials

We separated who gets content from when it’s due. Dates and availability vary by material and by student, and instructors told us they didn’t need them at the module level. Modules decide who, and dates stay on each material.

4. Shorten the path to individual assignment

Instructors still manage single materials, so we also put Assign To on the Modules page and the assignment Summary page instead of three pages deep.

Working through exceptions

Most of the complexity sat in the rules, not the screens.

I documented edge cases across module, section and student assignments, proposed behavior for each, and reviewed them with product and engineering.

One gap remained. There was no Modules-page overview of who is assigned to what, which matters more when items can differ from their module.

Outcome

From 150 clicks to 6

Ten materials in one module, assigned to the same students.

A 96% reduction in clicks.

The bigger change was what it made practical. Tailoring content across many materials had been tedious enough that instructors might not try. Assigning the whole module removes that friction.

After launch

The feature shipped after I left Instructure, so I didn’t measure adoption. Instructors were asking for it before release.

I can’t wait to see this feature enabled! I have been hoping for a feature like this since I started using Canvas back in 2020.

To evaluate it, I’d start with the share of courses assigning modules to specific students or sections, then check whether instructors understand how inheritance works.

Reflection

Define the decision criteria before testing

Setting the sample size and decision criteria up front would have made clear whether the study was choosing an approach or informing one.

Follow up on mixed signals

The tray’s higher misclick rate deserved a look at the click maps before deciding, to tell exploration from confusion.

Test the rules, not only the controls

A stronger study would also check whether instructors can predict what happens when a module and an item disagree.

Make exceptions visible

Keeping item-level control was right, but control needs visibility. The next problem is helping instructors see who can access what.
